Question 1(a)(i): What is the Global Reporting Initiatives (GRI) system?

Answer:
The Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) is a nonprofit organization that introduced a sustainability reporting framework in 2000. It provides a generally accepted framework for reporting on an organization’s economic, environmental, and social performance. The GRI system is designed to be used by organizations of any size, sector, or location. Its multi-stakeholder approach gives the framework credibility, and it emphasizes stakeholder inclusiveness, transparency, and materiality. The GRI Standards (launched in 2016) help organizations shape their sustainability strategy and report their practices.

Question 1(a)(ii): Describe statements of GRI’s (GRI-1, GRI-2, GRI-3) Universal Standards.

Answer:
The GRI Universal Standards (2021 update) consist of three standards:

  • GRI 1: Foundation 2021 – Introduces the purpose and key concepts of the GRI framework, including the principles for defining report content and quality. It explains how to use the standards and the requirement to report in accordance with them.

  • GRI 2: General Disclosures 2021 – Contains disclosures about the organization’s profile, strategy, ethics and integrity, governance, stakeholder engagement practices, and reporting process. Examples include organizational details, governance structure, and stakeholder engagement approaches.

  • GRI 3: Material Topics 2021 – Provides guidance on how to determine material topics and how to manage them. It requires disclosure of the process for identifying material topics, the list of material topics, and how each topic is managed (including policies, commitments, and actions).

(Note: The exam references GRI-1, GRI-2, GRI-3 as Universal Standards – this matches the 2021 revision. The chapter text mentions “GRI Standards version 2016” but the concept is consistent.)

Question 1(b): Describe 10 principles of UNGC (United Nations Global Compact).

Answer:
The UN Global Compact has ten principles grouped into four areas:

Human Rights

  1. Businesses should support and respect the protection of internationally proclaimed human rights.
  2. Make sure they are not complicit in human rights abuses.

Labour
3. Businesses should uphold the freedom of association and the effective recognition of the right to collective bargaining.
4. The elimination of all forms of forced and compulsory labour.
5. The effective abolition of child labour.
6. The elimination of discrimination in respect of employment and occupation.

Environment
7. Businesses should support a precautionary approach to environmental challenges.
8. Undertake initiatives to promote greater environmental responsibility.
9. Encourage the development and diffusion of environmentally friendly technologies.

Anti‑Corruption
10. Businesses should work against corruption in all its forms, including extortion and bribery.

Question 2(a): Discuss initiatives of ‘Intel’ and ‘Barclays’ for reducing carbon foot‑printing.

Answer:

Intel’s initiatives:

  • Recognized climate change as a problem requiring broad societal contribution and set aggressive targets.
  • By 2018, reduced Scope 1 and 2 emissions by ~40% on an absolute basis.
  • 2020 goal: reduce direct GHG emissions by 10% per unit from 2010 levels while expanding manufacturing.
  • Prioritizes energy reduction, investing in renewable energy and alternative sources.
  • Cooperated with the semiconductor industry to abolish Class 1 ozone‑depleting substances (1990s) and significantly reduce fluorinated gases.
  • Uses thermal oxidants and wet scrubbers to minimise VOCs, HAPs, NOx, and CO.
  • From 2012 to 2018, invested over US$185 million in 2,000 energy‑saving projects.
  • Incorporated energy efficiency into corporate upgrades (e.g., heat recovery chillers/heat pumps to reduce fossil fuel use for heating).

Barclays’ initiatives:

  • Adopted a group‑wide environmental management system to minimise direct operational impact.
  • Offsets remaining emissions by buying carbon credits.
  • In 2010, purchased 1,191,956 carbon credits from projects in Brazil, China, India, South Korea, Tanzania, Kenya, and Thailand.
  • Example: Brazil project – over 1,000 hectares of former cattle grazing land reforested with >50 endemic species, restoring biodiversity and providing local employment and training. That project alone generated 15,000 carbon credits.
  • Offsetting not only stores carbon but also delivers social and environmental co‑benefits (e.g., restoring Brazilian rainforest, which loses 30,000–40,000 km² per year).

Question 2©: What are classified three emissions scopes of GHG (Greenhouse Gas) Protocol?

Answer:
The GHG Protocol classifies emissions into three scopes:

  • Scope 1: Direct GHG emissions – Emissions from sources that are owned or controlled by the organization (e.g., company vehicles, on‑site fuel combustion, industrial processes).

  • Scope 2: Indirect GHG emissions from purchased energy – Emissions from the generation of purchased electricity, steam, heat, or cooling consumed by the organization.

  • Scope 3: Other indirect GHG emissions – All other indirect emissions that occur in the organization’s value chain (both upstream and downstream). Examples include: extraction of raw materials, employee business travel, waste disposal, use of sold products, supply chain activities, and investments.
